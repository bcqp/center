Statistical report

Agriculture, food production and prices in Bangladesh

Peer benchmarks and 5 model specifications on 12 variables (1991–2024) for food production, yields, prices and growth. Bangladesh is below the peer median and worsening on 1 of 4 indicators judged.

Tuhin G. M. Al Mamun and Rahim Md Abdur · Statistical report · 2026-11-14

Cite as: Al Mamun, Tuhin G. M., and Rahim Md Abdur (2026). Agriculture, food production and prices in Bangladesh. BIQEP Policy Briefs. Archived at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24215521.

Abstract. Have food production and crop yields kept up, and how do they shape inflation? We benchmark Bangladesh against India, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, Viet Nam, Indonesia and Nepal using World Bank data, and estimate a 12-variable Bayesian VAR with vector autoregression and local-projection checks on annual data for 1991–2024. Of 5 estimated impulse responses, 0 have a band that excludes zero at the peak. Where the models cannot separate the effect from zero, we say so.

Key findings

  • CPI inflation, %: Bangladesh 8.77 (2025), peer median 2.53; rank 7 of 7; change over ten years +2.57 (peer median -3.48).
  • GDP per head, constant US$: Bangladesh 1985 (2025), peer median 3416; rank 5 of 7; change over ten years +760 (peer median +660).
  • Food production index (2014-16 = 100): Bangladesh 135 (2024), peer median 121; rank 2 of 7; change over ten years +38.36 (peer median +23.31).
  • Cereal yield, kg per hectare: Bangladesh 5408 (2024), peer median 3850; rank 3 of 7; change over ten years +849 (peer median +540).
  • Models: the response of CPI inflation to a shock in food production index, growth is of the same sign across specifications; 0 of 5 bands exclude zero at the peak (Table 1).

1. Question

Have food production and crop yields kept up, and how do they shape inflation? We answer with official data, benchmarking against six regional peers and several estimators that make different assumptions; the results are reported whatever they show.

2. Data

Annual series from the World Bank World Development Indicators for Bangladesh, retrieved on 2026-11-14, years 1991–2024 (34 observations after transformation). The 12 variables are: Food production index, growth (%); CPI inflation (%); Real GDP growth (%); Cereal yield, growth (%); Change in agriculture/GDP (pp); Change in imports/GDP (pp); Depreciation of the Taka (%); Change in broad money/GDP (pp); Private consumption growth (%); Change in lending rate (pp); Change in investment/GDP (pp); Change in exports/GDP (pp). Transformations: growth rates and percentage changes where the series is a level, first differences where it is a ratio.

Validation. Single compiled source for most series. Real GDP growth and CPI inflation were compared with the IMF DataMapper series for 2015–2025 (IMF: NGDP_RPCH, PCPIPCH); see the data-validation note in the Statistical report on inflation, the Taka and policy stance for the gaps found (up to 0.05 percentage points for GDP growth and 1.6 for inflation). Gaps in peer data are shown with the year used.

4. Empirical strategy

We trace the response of CPI inflation to a shock in food production index, growth. Models, all run in Open Econometric Systems: (i) a Bayesian VAR with a Minnesota prior on 12 variables (shock ordered first), with a variation in lag length and prior tightness; (ii) a four-variable VAR with a recursive ordering; (iii) local projections with Newey–West standard errors. The large system is possible only in the Bayesian model: an unrestricted VAR on 12 variables with two lags has 25 coefficients per equation for about 34 usable observations, leaving too few degrees of freedom for reliable estimates. Recursive identification is an assumption; the responses are dynamic correlations conditional on the ordering, not causal effects.

5. Results

Figure 3 shows the response to a one-standard-deviation shock (sd = 4.59, in the units of the shock series). The response one year after the shock is of the same sign across specifications. 0 of 5 bands exclude zero at the largest response (Table 1). The BVAR bands are 68% credible bands and the VAR and local-projection bands are 95%, so they should not be compared directly.

6. Policy assessment

Where the record supports criticism

Bangladesh is more than 5% below the peer median, and its ten-year change is worse than the peer median change.

  1. CPI inflation, %: Bangladesh 8.77 (2025), peer median 2.53; rank 7 of 7; change over ten years +2.57 (peer median -3.48).

Behind peers, but improving

  • GDP per head, constant US$: Bangladesh 1985 (2025), peer median 3416; rank 5 of 7; change over ten years +760 (peer median +660).

Where Bangladesh does well

  • Food production index (2014-16 = 100): Bangladesh 135 (2024), peer median 121; rank 2 of 7; change over ten years +38.36 (peer median +23.31).
  • Cereal yield, kg per hectare: Bangladesh 5408 (2024), peer median 3850; rank 3 of 7; change over ten years +849 (peer median +540).

Where the evidence is not strong enough

  • Dynamic effects: 0 of 5 impulse-response bands exclude zero, so the estimated effect of food production index, growth on CPI inflation cannot be stated precisely with annual data.

Implications for discussion

Judgements drawn from the record, not estimates.

  • Yield growth relative to peers shows the room for catching up; yields, not area, are the margin for growth.
  • Food-price monitoring should be linked to production and import data so that supply and price signals are read together.

7. Limitations

  • Annual data give about 34 observations; responses at one to six years are fragile.
  • Recursive identification is an assumption, not a result.
  • Peer comparisons use each country's latest year, which differs for some indicators; ten-year changes use the year ten years before the latest.
  • Peer groups reflect economies of comparable region or size and are a choice; other groups could change ranks.
