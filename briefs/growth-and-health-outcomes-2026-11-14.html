Statistical report

Income growth and health outcomes in Bangladesh

Peer benchmarks and 5 model specifications on 12 variables (1991–2024) for income, mortality, life expectancy and structure. Bangladesh is below the peer median and worsening on 3 of 5 indicators judged.

Tuhin G. M. Al Mamun · Statistical report · 2026-11-14

Abstract. Have health outcomes improved as fast as incomes, and how responsive are they to growth? We benchmark Bangladesh against India, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, Viet Nam, Indonesia and Nepal using World Bank data, and estimate a 12-variable Bayesian VAR with vector autoregression and local-projection checks on annual data for 1991–2024. Of 5 estimated impulse responses, 0 have a band that excludes zero at the peak. Where the models cannot separate the effect from zero, we say so.

Key findings

  • Infant mortality, per 1,000 births: Bangladesh 24.40 (2024), peer median 18.70; rank 6 of 7; change over ten years -6.80 (peer median -9.70).
  • Health expenditure, % of GDP: Bangladesh 2.17 (2023), peer median 3.51; rank 7 of 7; change over ten years -0.13 (peer median +0.05).
  • Out-of-pocket share of health spending, %: Bangladesh 79.31 (2023), peer median 48.41; rank 7 of 7; change over ten years +9.69 (peer median -6.98).
  • GDP per head, constant US$: Bangladesh 1985 (2025), peer median 3416; rank 5 of 7; change over ten years +760 (peer median +660).
  • Life expectancy, years: Bangladesh 74.93 (2024), peer median 71.76; rank 2 of 7; change over ten years +4.91 (peer median +2.21).
  • Models: the response of infant mortality to a shock in GDP per capita growth is of mixed sign across specifications; 0 of 5 bands exclude zero at the peak (Table 1).

1. Question

Have health outcomes improved as fast as incomes, and how responsive are they to growth? We answer with official data, benchmarking against six regional peers and several estimators that make different assumptions; the results are reported whatever they show.

2. Data

Annual series from the World Bank World Development Indicators for Bangladesh, retrieved on 2026-11-14, years 1991–2024 (34 observations after transformation). The 12 variables are: GDP per capita growth (%); Change in infant mortality (per 1,000); Change in life expectancy (years); Real GDP growth (%); Change in urban population share (pp); Electricity use per head, growth (%); Change in agriculture/GDP (pp); Change in female labour participation (pp); Food production index, growth (%); CPI inflation (%); Change in investment/GDP (pp); Change in exports/GDP (pp). Transformations: growth rates and percentage changes where the series is a level, first differences where it is a ratio.

Validation. Single compiled source for most series. Real GDP growth and CPI inflation were compared with the IMF DataMapper series for 2015–2025 (IMF: NGDP_RPCH, PCPIPCH); see the data-validation note in the Statistical report on inflation, the Taka and policy stance for the gaps found (up to 0.05 percentage points for GDP growth and 1.6 for inflation). Gaps in peer data are shown with the year used.

4. Empirical strategy

We trace the response of infant mortality to a shock in GDP per capita growth. Models, all run in Open Econometric Systems: (i) a Bayesian VAR with a Minnesota prior on 12 variables (shock ordered first), with a variation in lag length and prior tightness; (ii) a four-variable VAR with a recursive ordering; (iii) local projections with Newey–West standard errors. The large system is possible only in the Bayesian model: an unrestricted VAR on 12 variables with two lags has 25 coefficients per equation for about 34 usable observations, leaving too few degrees of freedom for reliable estimates. Recursive identification is an assumption; the responses are dynamic correlations conditional on the ordering, not causal effects.

Figure 3 shows the response to a one-standard-deviation shock (sd = 1.47, in the units of the shock series). 6. Policy assessment

Where the record supports criticism

Bangladesh is more than 5% below the peer median, and its ten-year change is worse than the peer median change.

  1. Infant mortality, per 1,000 births: Bangladesh 24.40 (2024), peer median 18.70; rank 6 of 7; change over ten years -6.80 (peer median -9.70).
  2. Health expenditure, % of GDP: Bangladesh 2.17 (2023), peer median 3.51; rank 7 of 7; change over ten years -0.13 (peer median +0.05).
  3. Out-of-pocket share of health spending, %: Bangladesh 79.31 (2023), peer median 48.41; rank 7 of 7; change over ten years +9.69 (peer median -6.98).

Behind peers, but improving

  • GDP per head, constant US$: Bangladesh 1985 (2025), peer median 3416; rank 5 of 7; change over ten years +760 (peer median +660).

Where Bangladesh does well

  • Life expectancy, years: Bangladesh 74.93 (2024), peer median 71.76; rank 2 of 7; change over ten years +4.91 (peer median +2.21).

Where the evidence is not strong enough

  • Dynamic effects: 0 of 5 impulse-response bands exclude zero, so the estimated effect of GDP per capita growth on infant mortality cannot be stated precisely with annual data.

Implications for discussion

Judgements drawn from the record, not estimates.

  • Total health spending and the out-of-pocket share should be reported together; a high private share shifts costs onto households.
  • Health outcome gains should be tracked against per-person spending to measure efficiency.

7. Limitations

  • Annual data give about 34 observations; responses at one to six years are fragile.
  • Recursive identification is an assumption, not a result.
  • Peer comparisons use each country's latest year, which differs for some indicators; ten-year changes use the year ten years before the latest.
  • Peer groups reflect economies of comparable region or size and are a choice; other groups could change ranks.
