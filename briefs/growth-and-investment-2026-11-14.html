Growth and investment in Bangladesh: how far does capital formation carry output?

Peer benchmarks and 5 model specifications on 12 variables (1991–2025) for investment, savings, FDI and growth. Bangladesh is below the peer median and worsening on 2 of 5 indicators judged.

Tuhin G. M. Al Mamun · Statistical report · 2026-11-14

Abstract. Is the pace of investment keeping up with the growth Bangladesh needs, and how strongly has investment fed into output? We benchmark Bangladesh against India, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, Viet Nam, Indonesia and Nepal using World Bank data, and estimate a 12-variable Bayesian VAR with vector autoregression and local-projection checks on annual data for 1991–2025. Of 5 estimated impulse responses, 0 have a band that excludes zero at the peak. Where the models cannot separate the effect from zero, we say so.

Key findings

  • Real GDP growth, %: Bangladesh 3.49 (2025), peer median 5.03; rank 7 of 7; change over ten years -3.06 (peer median +0.34).
  • FDI inflows, % of GDP: Bangladesh 0.39 (2025), peer median 1.00; rank 6 of 7; change over ten years -1.06 (peer median -0.39).
  • Investment, % of GDP: Bangladesh 28.54 (2025), peer median 30.13; rank 6 of 7; change over ten years -0.35 (peer median -1.70).
  • GDP per head, constant US$: Bangladesh 1985 (2025), peer median 3416; rank 5 of 7; change over ten years +760 (peer median +660).
  • Gross savings, % of GNI: Bangladesh 34.81 (2025), peer median 34.87; rank 4 of 7; change over ten years -1.92 (peer median +2.61).
  • Models: the response of real GDP growth to a shock in investment/GDP is of the same sign across specifications; 0 of 5 bands exclude zero at the peak (Table 1).

1. Question

Is the pace of investment keeping up with the growth Bangladesh needs, and how strongly has investment fed into output? We answer with official data, benchmarking against six regional peers and several estimators that make different assumptions; the results are reported whatever they show.

2. Data

Annual series from the World Bank World Development Indicators for Bangladesh, retrieved on 2026-11-14, years 1991–2025 (35 observations after transformation). The 12 variables are: Change in investment/GDP (pp); Real GDP growth (%); GDP per capita growth (%); Change in exports/GDP (pp); Change in private credit/GDP (pp); Change in gross savings/GNI (pp); Change in FDI inflows/GDP (pp); Change in broad money/GDP (pp); CPI inflation (%); Change in imports/GDP (pp); Depreciation of the Taka (%); Change in lending rate (pp). Transformations: growth rates and percentage changes where the series is a level, first differences where it is a ratio.

Validation. Single compiled source for most series. Real GDP growth and CPI inflation were compared with the IMF DataMapper series for 2015–2025 (IMF: NGDP_RPCH, PCPIPCH); see the data-validation note in the Statistical report on inflation, the Taka and policy stance for the gaps found (up to 0.05 percentage points for GDP growth and 1.6 for inflation). Gaps in peer data are shown with the year used.

4. Empirical strategy

We trace the response of real GDP growth to a shock in investment/GDP. Models, all run in Open Econometric Systems: (i) a Bayesian VAR with a Minnesota prior on 12 variables (shock ordered first), with a variation in lag length and prior tightness; (ii) a four-variable VAR with a recursive ordering; (iii) local projections with Newey–West standard errors. The large system is possible only in the Bayesian model: an unrestricted VAR on 12 variables with two lags has 25 coefficients per equation for about 35 usable observations, leaving too few degrees of freedom for reliable estimates. Recursive identification is an assumption; the responses are dynamic correlations conditional on the ordering, not causal effects.

5. Results

Figure 3 shows the response to a one-standard-deviation shock (sd = 0.70, in the units of the shock series). The response one year after the shock is of the same sign across specifications. 0 of 5 bands exclude zero at the largest response (Table 1). The BVAR bands are 68% credible bands and the VAR and local-projection bands are 95%, so they should not be compared directly.

6. Policy assessment

Where the record supports criticism

Bangladesh is more than 5% below the peer median, and its ten-year change is worse than the peer median change.

  1. Real GDP growth, %: Bangladesh 3.49 (2025), peer median 5.03; rank 7 of 7; change over ten years -3.06 (peer median +0.34).
  2. FDI inflows, % of GDP: Bangladesh 0.39 (2025), peer median 1.00; rank 6 of 7; change over ten years -1.06 (peer median -0.39).

Behind peers, but improving

  • Investment, % of GDP: Bangladesh 28.54 (2025), peer median 30.13; rank 6 of 7; change over ten years -0.35 (peer median -1.70).
  • GDP per head, constant US$: Bangladesh 1985 (2025), peer median 3416; rank 5 of 7; change over ten years +760 (peer median +660).

Where Bangladesh does well

  • Gross savings, % of GNI: Bangladesh 34.81 (2025), peer median 34.87; rank 4 of 7; change over ten years -1.92 (peer median +2.61).

Where the evidence is not strong enough

  • Dynamic effects: 0 of 5 impulse-response bands exclude zero, so the estimated effect of investment/GDP on real GDP growth cannot be stated precisely with annual data.

Implications for discussion

Judgements drawn from the record, not estimates.

  • Investment and FDI ratios should be read next to the peer group: the gap, not the level, is the policy question.
  • Tracking the investment ratio quarterly, with its public and private split, would show where the shortfall lies.

7. Limitations

  • Annual data give about 35 observations; responses at one to six years are fragile.
  • Recursive identification is an assumption, not a result.
  • Peer comparisons use each country's latest year, which differs for some indicators; ten-year changes use the year ten years before the latest.
  • Peer groups reflect economies of comparable region or size and are a choice; other groups could change ranks.
