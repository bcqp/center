Research framework

Quantitative policy analysis: from data to better understanding.

BIQEP uses economics, statistics, econometrics and mathematical modelling to investigate how policies work, why outcomes differ and what the available evidence implies for public decisions.

Our central purpose

BIQEP's purpose is to make complex economic and social policy questions more understandable through quantitative evidence. We do not treat econometric estimation as an end in itself. We begin with a substantive question, identify the economic or institutional mechanism that may explain it, and select a quantitative strategy capable of examining that mechanism.

Policy debates often rely on aggregate indicators, isolated correlations or short-term observations. These can be informative, but they may conceal differences across households, workers, firms, regions and generations. Quantitative policy research helps organise the evidence, measure the size of relationships, evaluate competing explanations and make uncertainty explicit.

Four connected layers of analysis

1. Descriptive measurement: what is happening?

We first establish the empirical facts. This includes constructing consistent indicators, examining trends and volatility, identifying turning points and structural breaks, comparing groups, and studying the distribution of outcomes. Descriptive analysis provides the measurement foundation for more demanding empirical work.

2. Explanatory analysis: how and why are outcomes changing?

We investigate relationships and transmission mechanisms. Time-series models can examine dynamic interactions among inflation, interest rates, exchange rates, credit and output. Panel models can compare countries, sectors or regions while allowing for heterogeneity and common shocks. Structural and state-space approaches can help represent latent conditions and economic mechanisms.

3. Causal and evaluative analysis: what difference did a policy make?

Where the research design and data permit, we estimate policy effects using causal inference and programme-evaluation methods. The analysis states the target estimand, comparison group, identifying assumptions and threats to identification. Difference-in-differences, event studies, instrumental-variable strategies and treatment-effect methods are used only when their assumptions can be examined and defended.

4. Scenario analysis: what could happen under alternative choices?

Forecasting and structural simulation can help compare possible paths under explicit assumptions. Scenario analysis is not a claim that the future is known. It is a disciplined way to ask how outcomes may change when policy settings, external conditions or behavioural responses differ.

Quantitative methods we draw upon

  • Econometric time-series analysis: ARDL and error-correction models, cointegration, VAR and VECM, local projections, state-space models and volatility analysis.
  • Panel-data econometrics: fixed- and random-effects models, dynamic panels, heterogeneous-slope estimators, common-correlated-effects approaches and cross-sectional dependence analysis.
  • Causal inference: difference-in-differences, event studies, instrumental variables, regression discontinuity and treatment-effect estimation, subject to design-specific identification conditions.
  • Structural economics: structural macroeconomic models, DSGE frameworks and counterfactual simulation for analysing transmission mechanisms and policy scenarios.
  • Statistical learning: regularised regression, tree-based methods, random forests and forecasting algorithms, evaluated using appropriate validation procedures.
  • Distributional analysis: quantile regression, inequality and poverty measures, subgroup analysis and assessment of heterogeneous policy effects.

Connecting macroeconomic policy with social outcomes

Economic policy is not separate from social welfare. Inflation affects real purchasing power; interest-rate changes influence borrowers, savers and firms differently; fiscal choices shape the availability and distribution of public services; labour-market changes affect income security; and energy transitions can create both new opportunities and adjustment costs.

BIQEP therefore seeks to connect macroeconomic indicators with the social outcomes behind them. For example, an analysis of energy transition should not stop at investment or emissions. It can also examine employment reallocation, household energy affordability, regional exposure, skills requirements, public revenue and the distribution of transition costs. Similarly, an inflation study should consider not only the aggregate rate but also food-price exposure, real wages and differences in household consumption patterns.

From estimates to policy discussion

Our policy discussion follows the evidence rather than preceding it. We interpret effect sizes in meaningful units, explain the time horizon, identify which groups or sectors are most exposed, and discuss the assumptions that matter for interpretation. Where findings are mixed or imprecise, that uncertainty is reported rather than hidden.

A policy brief should help a reader answer five questions: What is the problem? What does the data show? What mechanism or policy channel is being examined? How strong is the evidence? What are the implications and trade-offs for policy?

Transparency and reproducibility

Each quantitative study should document its data sources, definitions, sample period, transformations, missing-data treatment, model specification, identifying assumptions, diagnostics and robustness checks. Where permitted, replication materials should make it possible for readers to reproduce the reported estimates. Our Data Hub, methodology pages and publication policies support this standard.

Research focus

Our current research agenda includes inflation and monetary policy, public finance, labour and wages, external-sector adjustment, finance and credit, climate and energy, growth and structural transformation, and human development. These themes are connected by a common objective: use quantitative economics to understand policy problems and their consequences for society.
