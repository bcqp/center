Research framework

Quantitative policy analysis: from data to better understanding.

BIQEP uses economics, statistics, econometrics and mathematical modelling to investigate how policies work, why outcomes differ and what the available evidence implies for public decisions.

Our central purpose

BIQEP investigates economic and social policy questions through quantitative evidence. Estimation is not an end in itself: analysis begins with a substantive question, identifies the economic or institutional mechanism and selects a strategy capable of examining it. The objective is to clarify what the evidence establishes, how strongly it supports an interpretation and where uncertainty remains.

Aggregate indicators and isolated correlations can conceal differences across households, workers, firms, regions and generations. Quantitative policy research organises evidence, measures relationships, evaluates competing explanations and makes assumptions explicit. This approach is consistent with the emphasis on transparent statistical concepts and methods in the UN National Quality Assurance Framework and the IMF's statistical dissemination standards and metadata.

Four connected layers of analysis

1. Descriptive measurement: what is happening?

Establish empirical facts by constructing consistent indicators, examining trends and volatility, identifying turning points and structural breaks, comparing groups and studying outcome distributions. Descriptive analysis provides the measurement foundation for more demanding empirical work.

2. Explanatory analysis: how and why are outcomes changing?

Investigate relationships and transmission mechanisms. Time-series models examine dynamic interactions among inflation, interest rates, exchange rates, credit and output. Panel models compare countries, sectors or regions while allowing for heterogeneity and common shocks. Structural and state-space approaches represent latent conditions and economic mechanisms.

3. Causal and evaluative analysis: what difference did a policy make?

Where research design and data permit, estimate policy effects using causal inference and programme-evaluation methods. State the target estimand, treatment, comparison group, identifying assumptions and threats. Difference-in-differences, event studies, instrumental-variable strategies and treatment-effect methods require design-specific justification; see the American Economic Association's data and code policy as a reference for transparent empirical documentation, not as a certification of BIQEP work.

4. Scenario analysis: what could happen under alternative choices?

Forecasting and structural simulation compare possible paths under explicit assumptions. A scenario is a conditional exercise, not a claim that the future is known. The analysis should identify the horizon, baseline, policy assumptions and sources of uncertainty.

Quantitative methods

  • Econometric time-series analysis: ARDL and error-correction models, cointegration, VAR/VECM, local projections, state-space models and volatility analysis.
  • Panel-data econometrics: fixed- and random-effects models, dynamic panels, heterogeneous-slope estimators, common-correlated-effects approaches and cross-sectional dependence analysis.
  • Causal inference: difference-in-differences, event studies, instrumental variables, regression discontinuity and treatment-effect estimation, subject to identification conditions.
  • Structural economics: structural macroeconomic models and counterfactual simulation for transmission mechanisms and policy scenarios.
  • Statistical learning: regularised regression, tree-based methods, random forests and forecasting algorithms, evaluated with appropriate validation.
  • Distributional analysis: quantile regression, inequality and poverty measures, subgroup analysis and heterogeneous policy effects.

Connecting macroeconomic policy with social outcomes

Inflation affects real purchasing power; interest-rate changes influence borrowers, savers and firms differently; fiscal choices shape public-service provision; labor-market changes affect income security; and energy transitions create both opportunities and adjustment costs. BIQEP therefore links macroeconomic indicators to the social outcomes behind them.

An energy-transition assessment, for example, should not stop at investment or emissions. It can examine employment reallocation, household energy affordability, regional exposure, skills requirements, public revenue and the distribution of transition costs. The UN SDG indicator metadata provides definitions for relevant energy, employment and development measures; the ILO's ILOSTAT provides labor-market series and metadata.

From estimates to policy discussion

Policy discussion follows the evidence. Interpret effect sizes in meaningful units, explain the time horizon, identify exposed groups or sectors and discuss assumptions. Where findings are mixed or imprecise, report that uncertainty. Each brief should answer: What is the problem? What does the data show? Which mechanism is examined? How strong is the evidence? What are the implications and trade-offs?

Transparency and reproducibility

Studies document data sources, definitions, sample period, transformations, missing-data treatment, model specification, identifying assumptions, diagnostics and robustness checks. Where permitted, replication materials should support reproduction of reported estimates. See the Data Hub, methodology pages and publication policies.

Research focus

Current themes include inflation and monetary policy, public finance, labor and wages, external-sector adjustment, finance and credit, climate and energy, growth and structural transformation, and human development.
