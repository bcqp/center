Research area

Climate and energy

Energy prices, green finance and climate risk.

Why climate and energy matter

Energy systems connect macroeconomic stability, industrial competitiveness, public finance, household welfare and environmental sustainability. Energy-price changes can pass through production costs and consumer prices; energy subsidies and tariff adjustments affect fiscal space and distribution; climate risks can damage infrastructure, livelihoods and productive capacity. A just-transition perspective therefore asks not only how the energy system changes, but who bears costs and who gains from investment and new employment.

Research questions

  • How do fuel and electricity price shocks affect inflation, output and sectoral production?
  • What are the fiscal, quasi-fiscal and distributional effects of energy subsidies and tariff reforms?
  • How do climate hazards affect agricultural output, labor income, infrastructure and regional development?
  • Does green finance reach sectors and firms that contribute to measurable transition outcomes?
  • How are employment, skills, affordability and energy access affected during decarbonisation?

Data and measurement

Relevant evidence may combine official energy price and production data, BBS economic and household statistics [Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics (BBS)], macroeconomic series from Bangladesh Bank, Economic Data, and environmental and development indicators from World Bank, World Development Indicators. The UN SDG indicator metadata provides definitions and metadata for selected energy, climate, employment and development indicators. Data should be matched by geography, frequency and reference period; climate hazards, energy use, emissions and finance are distinct constructs.

Analytical framework

BIQEP connects energy supply and prices to firm costs, household energy burdens, public budgets and labor-market adjustment. Distributional analysis should consider household expenditure shares, income groups, urban-rural differences, gender and employment exposure. Green-finance analysis should define eligible instruments and uses of proceeds rather than infer environmental impact from a label alone.

Methods and identification

  • Input-output and sectoral exposure analysis: indirect cost channels across production networks, subject to table availability and vintage.
  • Local projections and structural VARs: macroeconomic responses to energy-price or climate shocks under explicit identification.
  • Panel and threshold models: heterogeneous effects across regions, sectors or exposure levels.
  • Distributional and labor-market analysis: incidence across households and workers using suitable microdata.

Climate exposure is not randomly assigned, and policy adoption may be endogenous. Causal claims require a design that addresses confounding, selection and spatial spillovers. Results should distinguish physical climate risk, transition risk and policy-induced price changes. Scenario results are conditional on assumptions about technology, demand, prices and policy.

Policy relevance

Research can inform energy affordability, targeted compensation, subsidy reform, renewable investment, green finance standards, skills policy and regional transition planning. A just-transition assessment should report employment quality, distributional incidence and access to affordable energy alongside emissions or investment indicators.
