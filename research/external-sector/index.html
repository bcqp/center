Research area

External sector

Exchange rate, remittances, reserves and trade.

Why the external sector matters

Trade, remittances, external financing, exchange rates and reserves connect domestic production and consumption to global markets. External shocks can affect imported inflation, foreign-currency liquidity, public and private balance sheets and the availability of inputs. A sound assessment therefore considers flows, stocks, prices and financing conditions together.

Research questions

  • How do exchange-rate changes affect import prices, consumer prices and export competitiveness?
  • How do remittance flows respond to exchange-rate incentives, labor-market conditions and policy changes?
  • What do reserve adequacy and external debt indicators reveal about external liquidity risks?
  • How do export and import volumes adjust after large relative-price changes?
  • How do current-account and financial-account developments interact?

Data and measurement

Bangladesh Bank publishes exchange-rate, remittance and balance-of-payments information through Bangladesh Bank, Economic Data; the Bangladesh National Summary Data Page provides access to balance of payments, external debt, reserve assets, merchandise trade, international investment position and exchange rates. Cross-country financial and external-sector comparisons may use IMF Data and World Bank, World Development Indicators. IMF dissemination metadata helps document definitions and compilation practices [IMF Dissemination Standards Bulletin Board].

Analysis should distinguish gross from net flows, stocks from transactions, calendar from fiscal years, nominal from real exchange rates, and official from market exchange-rate concepts where relevant. Reserve measures need clear coverage, valuation and encumbrance treatment. Trade values should be separated from quantities and unit values where possible.

Analytical framework

BCQP examines exchange-rate pass-through, external adjustment, remittance behavior, trade elasticities, reserve adequacy and external debt dynamics. The framework should identify whether the question concerns price adjustment, quantity adjustment, financing constraints or balance-sheet exposure.

Methods and identification

  • VAR and local projections: dynamic responses to external and domestic shocks under stated identification assumptions.
  • Nonlinear ARDL: possible asymmetry between exchange-rate appreciations and depreciations, where integration and bounds-test conditions are met.
  • Structural-break and regime tests: changes in relationships across policy or market regimes.
  • Panel methods: comparative evidence across countries, with cross-sectional dependence and heterogeneity addressed.

External shocks may be endogenous to domestic conditions, and exchange-rate regimes can change the interpretation of estimated responses. Results should test alternative exchange-rate measures, break dates, sample windows and shock definitions. Wavelet or frequency-domain tools describe co-movement across frequencies; they do not independently identify causal effects.

Policy relevance

Evidence can inform exchange-rate management, reserve policy, export diversification, remittance channels and external-debt risk monitoring. Policy conclusions should distinguish short-run liquidity pressures from longer-run competitiveness and productivity constraints.
