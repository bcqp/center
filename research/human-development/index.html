Research area

Human development

Health, education and living standards in relation to income.

Why human development matters

Economic growth translates into improved living standards through health, education, nutrition, housing, sanitation, social protection and access to opportunity. Average income or output can conceal large differences across regions and population groups. Human-development analysis therefore combines outcome levels with distribution, service access, quality and public or household financing.

Research questions

  • Have health, education and living-standard outcomes improved alongside income growth?
  • How do public and out-of-pocket health expenditures relate to financial protection?
  • How do outcomes differ across gender, location, income groups and other relevant populations?
  • What is the relationship between education, skills, labor-market outcomes and productivity?
  • How do macroeconomic shocks and price changes affect household welfare and access to essential services?

Data and measurement

Bangladesh's official statistical system publishes demographic, social and economic information through Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics (BBS); international comparisons can use World Bank, World Development Indicators and the UN SDG indicator metadata. The UN metadata repository documents indicator definitions, units, disaggregation and responsible agencies for Sustainable Development Goal indicators [UNSD, SDG metadata; UN SDG indicator metadata].

Researchers should distinguish access from utilization, coverage from quality, nominal expenditure from real expenditure, and individual outcomes from household-level measures. Survey estimates require attention to weights, strata, clusters, nonresponse and comparability across waves. Administrative service data may reflect reporting capacity as well as actual service delivery.

Analytical framework

BCQP studies the links among income, public-service provision, household expenditure, health and education outcomes, and distributional inequality. A multidimensional perspective is useful because improvements in one indicator do not necessarily imply progress in all dimensions. Where possible, outcomes should be disaggregated by sex, age, location and socioeconomic position using definitions consistent with the source.

Methods and identification

  • Survey-weighted descriptive analysis: outcome distributions and access gaps.
  • Decomposition methods: accounting for observed group differences, with assumptions stated.
  • Panel and repeated-cross-section models: trends and conditional associations across survey waves.
  • Quasi-experimental evaluation: policy impacts where a credible treatment assignment and comparison strategy exist.

Cross-sectional associations do not establish causal effects. Evaluations should specify treatment, comparison group, outcome, timing, identifying assumptions and threats such as spillovers or selective take-up. Results should report uncertainty and distributional heterogeneity, not only average effects.

Policy relevance

Research can inform human-capital investment, health financing, education quality, social protection and equitable service delivery. Recommendations should distinguish supply constraints from affordability and access barriers and should state which groups are expected to benefit.
